Zeferino Laboratory of Embedded and Distributed Systems – LEDS University of Vale do Itajaí – UNIVALI Itajaí, Santa Catarina, Brazil {lucasmartins,sborzguilherme}@edu.univali.br,{viel,zeferino}@univali.br ABSTRACT Hyperspectral images (HSIs) have been used in civil and military scenarios for ground recognition, urban development management, rare minerals identification, and diverse other purposes. However, HSIs have a significant volume of information and require high computational power, especially for real-time processing in embedded applications, as in onboard computers in satellites. These issues have driven the development of hardware-based solutions able to provide the processing power necessary to meet such requirements. In this paper, we present a hardware accelerator to enhance the performance of one of the most computational expensive stages of HSI processing: the classification. We have employed the Entropy Multiple Correlation Ratio procedure to select the spectral bands to be used in the training process. For the classification step, we have applied a Support Vector Machine classifier with a Hamming Distance decision approach. The proposed custom processor was implemented in FPGA and compared with high-level implementations. The results obtained demonstrate that the processor has a silicon cost lower than similar solutions and can perform a realtime pixel classification in 0.1 ms and achieves a state-of-the-art accuracy of 99.7%. ACM ISBN 978-1-4503-6844-5/19/08. . .$15.00 https://doi.org/10.1145/3338852.33398691 INTRODUCTION The hyperspectral imaging concept was introduced at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) during the development of a system nowadays called AVIRIS (the acronym for the Airborne Visible InfraRed Imaging Spectrometer). This system became a platform primarily used in the field of landscape image acquisition. It is capable of acquiring images in a spectral region from 400 to 2500 nanometers, using 224 continuous spectral channels (also called bands) with a nominal spectral resolution of 10 nanometers [ 1],[2]. With the advance in the employment of hyperspectral imaging sensors, future space mission will require embedded systems with a high computational power to deal with an increasing volume of data. In contrast, onboard storage capacity and downlink bandwidth technologies of satellites are not keeping up with the acquisition capacity of the new instruments [ 3],[4]. The causes of this bottleneck are due to the structure of the HSIs. This characteristic makes the image size to be dependent on the number of spectral bands acquired, and the transmission of a hyperspectral image (HSI) through a satellite downlink can require a considerable amount of time [ 3]. One alternative to overcome these constraints relies on the onboard processing of the HSIs, which can reduce the amount of storage or the time necessary to transmit the information obtained from the image (e.g., image characteristics). However, new computational techniques are necessary to process an HSI in real-time [ 5], as well as hardware/software platforms for onboard processing. In this sense, the present work seeks a method to achieve a realtime onboard classification of HSIs, besides assessing the impact of different sets of classes in the classification process. Similarly to other authors who have addressed this problem, we employed Support Vector Machine (SVM) to implement an accurate hardware accelerator for synthesis in FPGA but also focusing on reducing the logic resources consumption. Moreover, we have addressed the band selection process in a more detailed way, applying Entropy Multiple Correlation Ratio (EMCR), a technique proposed by [ 6]. This aggregation of techniques resulted in a coprocessor with classification accuracy of 99.7% and capable of real-time processing for the AVIRIS acquisition technology. The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 discusses other works that implement HSI classifiers in hardware. Section 3 describes the solution proposed and techniques employed in this work, while Section 4 presents and discusses the design process and the experimental results. Zeferino 2 RELATED WORK Many works have proposed an onboard HSI analysis approach to overcome the constraints mentioned above. In [ 7], the authors have presented an HSI classification based on edge-preserving filtering. This approach selects a classification map to generate a guide image and defines every class of pixel using its maximum probability factor. Similarly, in [ 8], the authors have proposed an extraction technique also using the edge limits (or boundaries) of the image but applying a different algorithm. Firstly, a set of support vectors (SVs) is created using an SVM. This set of SVs is then applied to define the training edge samples in the image. After the training, the image characteristics are extracted using discriminant analysis. On this subject, in [ 9], the authors have described an HSI processing and interpretation solution for an embedded system. This solution was prototyped with a massively parallel many-core technology based on Ramon Chips ITAR free RC64 architecture. In [ 10], the authors have proposed a high-efficiency HSI classification solution based on SVM and Hamming Distance for implementation in FPGA. The architecture comprises three main steps: (i ) subtraction circuits to compute the pixel data and generate a resulting code; (ii ) Hamming distance to compare the resulting code with all the other known classes; and (iii ) the definition of the class of each pixel. Although the works above have achieved a high accuracy solution, none of those studies described the classes used nor assessed its influence on the solution. Furthermore, although such solutions have presented high accuracy (from 95 to 98%), only in [ 10] the proposed accelerator can carry out the classification process in realtime but at the cost of spending most of the resources of the chosen FPGA. Similarly to [ 10], we have developed a custom processor capable of processing an HSI in real-time. Our solution explores spatial parallelism through the use of multiple instances of a low-cost processor and offers greater accuracy at a lower total cost. 3 ARCHITECTURAL DESIGN 3.1 Dataset To validate the solution, we have employed the Indian Pines dataset, which was acquired using the AVIRIS and provided by the University of Basque Country [ 11]. This image is a 145 ×145 pixels photography taken from a mixed agriculture/forestry area in Northwestern Indiana on June 1992 [ 10]. This dataset contains 16 different classes. Similarly to the authors mentioned before [ 10],[12], we have employed smaller groups of classes to simplify the development of the proposed coprocessor. In this work, we have chosen to work with five different classes at a time. 3.1.1 Classes. To assess the influence of the classes chosen in the classification process, we have defined two smaller groups of classes among the 16 available in the dataset. These two groups are composed of five classes each. The classes of the first group were chosen based on its level of similarity. The purpose of this group was to evaluate how well SVMs can handle similar classes. This group, which from now we are going to address as group 01, comprises (1) Soybean-mintill (i.e., with minimal tillage); (2) Corn; (3) Soybean-notill (i.e., with zero tillage); (4) Hay-windrowed; and (5) Woods. It is important tonotice that the only difference between classes 1 and 3 is the way the soil was prepared. The second group (group 02) was chosen in the inverse way used in group 01. It means we have selected the classes based on the lowest level of similarity among them. Group 02 comprises (1) Corn-notill; (2) Grass-Trees; (3) Wheat; (4) Hay-windrowed; and (5) Woods. The Hay-windrowed and Woods classes were kept from the first group. We have decided to keep these classes in both groups because they presented to be easily distinguishable at the initial tests. This choice is also justified because this second group aims at assessing the classification performance with very different classes. 3.1.2 Bands. Hyperspectral images are usually composed of a vast number of bands, which is also referred to as the image spectral dimensionality. The Indian Pines dataset contains 220 spectral bands, what means that our dataset would form a hypercube of 145×145×220. This high dimensionality might be a problem when dealing with the classification process. According to [ 13], there is a limitation of classification power caused by the dimensionality of the images, once the training samples are limited to a fixed number. This issue is also known as the Hughes phenomenon [ 6]. This phenomenon have led us to choose a reduced number of bands to be applied to the training and classification process. While we needed to reduce the number of bands to overcome the Hughes phenomenon, we had to take care to select classes which were valuable for the prediction step. Based on previous works published that have used this same dataset as we did, it is known that we should take out the water absorption bands from the dataset. This removal of bands was the first reduction applied to our dataset, which lowered the number of bands to 200. From this point, all the bands were presumably valuable to the image classification. However, we had to perform a band selection to reduce the number of bands further and took into account the similarity and informativeness of each band. We have decided to apply the Entropy Multiple Correlation Ratio (EMCR) procedure [6] to perform this task. The EMCR procedure is a method of unsupervised feature selection that allows the reduction of the number of bands while keeping the high accuracy to the classification process [ 6]. This method relies on entropy for a search for bands of high informativeness while applying correlation to prevent the selection of similar bands. The algorithm is described on the steps below [6]: Step 1. First of all, we have needed to define the number of bands we were looking for. We needed to find a number of bands which was great enough to make it possible to distinguish the classes and at the same time, small enough to prevent the training to become too complex at the point of the precision becoming worse. We have defined our number of bands b=10from the 220 available. Step 2. Although we have had already removed the water absorption bands from the dataset, some of the remaining bands can be considered noisy. To isolate these bands, we have carried out a correlation analysis among the bands, which is shown in Fig. 1. Bands with a correlation lower than the threshold (represented by the red line) are considered noisy and were not taken into account. The primary input of the multi-classifier is the pixel data, and every pixel has a value addressed to each one of the ten different spectral bands. So, we can represent a pixel as being an array of ten 32-bits values. The result code is a binary code with whose width equals the number of binary classifiers used (i.e., 10 bits). A classifier is responsible for defining a single bit of the result code which corresponds to a pair of classes. That is, every classifier, which corresponds to a pair of classes aandb, decides to each one of these classes the input data is most similar. We have first described the algorithm in MATLAB®and C languages. After validation, the C code was manually converted to an RTL (Register-transfer Level) design with the use of VHDL to implement a synthesizable hardware model. To validate the efficiency of the implemented solutions, we have tested the classification process on multiple platforms. The test consisted of the classification of a block of pixels from the Indian Pines dataset. The training process was performed in MATLAB to obtain the parameters for the classifiers. We have then employed these parameters to evaluate the solution on three different platforms: a PC, an embedded processor, and the hardware accelerator. The experiments compared five different designs. The first two were the MATLAB and the C codes executing on Linux on a PC with an Intel®i7-4720 running at 2.6 GHz. The third design comprised a C code running on an ARM Cortex-A9 Dual-Core running at 800 MHz. Finally, we have evaluated the hardware accelerator synthesized to an Altera Cyclone®V SE 5CSEMA5F31C6N SoC FPGA device available in the Terasic DE1 SoC Development Kit. We have implemented two designs in the programmable logic area. One with a single classification processor (single-core) and another with six parallel processors (hexa-core). These last two designs aimed to evaluate the resources necessary to meet the AVIRIS requirements (i.e., 123.1 µs per pixel [ 10]). The maximum operating frequency have equaled 200 MHz for the single-core and 165 MHz for the hexa-core. For the training process, we have employed 100 samples from each class and chosen 200 random samples for every one of the classes for testing. These 1,000 samples were processed and classified sequentially to verify accuracy. We have conducted this process for both sets of classes. Tables 2 and 3 summarize the experimental results. For the group containing similar classes (Table 2), all the designs have obtained an average accuracy of 94.1%. For the second group, the classification accuracy equals an average of 99.7%. Table 4 presents the processing time that each design spends to classify a single pixel. Results show that the hardware accelerator containing a single processor classifies the pixel as faster as the PC and runs at a clock frequency 13 times slower. However, this design does not meet the AVIRIS requirements for real-time operation.
