DFVLR, NE-OE, Oberpfaffenhofen,F.R.G. Abstract The ROSIS imaging spectrometer concept is based on all reflective optics and matrix CCD detector arrays. The instrument concept was defined and detailed within a national study for chlorophyll measurements from space platforms, which was the design determining mission objective in terms of high spectral and radiometric and moderate spatial resolution. A great variety of further mission applications in the field of ocean, land and atmospheric remote sensing is foreseen. Meanwhile, a first airborne prototype version is under development for delivery in late 1988, funded commonly by DFVLR, GKSS and MBB, which will be used to verify the technical sensor concept of ROSIS and its full application spectrum. ROSIS is designed to cover a spectral range from 430 to 960 nm in resolution steps of 5 nm per channel; a set of up to 28 spectral channels can be read out simultaneously at full spatial resolution, the full spectrum at reduced resolution. The selection of channels can be pre-programmed per orbit or aircraft flight out of the available 106 channels. The radiometric resolution is defined as 0.05% of the apparent albedo. The total FOV covers ± 16° per optics module. In view of the ESA (and NASA) planning for Polar Platform Missions, ROSIS could represent a promising candidate of mul­ ti-purpose remote sensing instruments. 1. Introduction During the past 5 years, a new generation of optical remote sensing sensors has evolved under the designation of "imaging spec­ trometers", precisely describing their principle feature which is to acquire image data from moving platforms such as aircraft and space platforms in two dimensions: spatial (across track) and spectral "scanning". This means, a scan line on ground is represented in a great number of spectral lines through a spectrometer (dispersive element) and imaged onto a 2-dimensional detector array. The basic principles have been published in various symposia, proceedings and technical publications, thus a repetition is not necessary within this paper. The functional description in the following paragraph should enable the principle understanding of an imaging spectrometer anyhow. The existing imaging spectrometers all airborne versions and the planned instruments (see Ref. /3/ to /6/) are either based on refractive lens optics, such as the Canadian FLI (/3/) or small FOV reflective optical concepts. Since the ROSIS defining mission parameters, see Tab. 1, asked for a medium FOV and a rather high radiometric resolution - originating from the task to measure the narrow band, low amplitude chlorophyll fluorescence from space platform altitudes a new all-reflective optical concept has been designed to meet these general requirements. This exhibits the fundamental ad­ vantage to cover the total spectral range with one optics module and, for a later development stage, to expand the spectral range into the IR, especially to the SWIR range at 1.2 to 2.4 //m with a high application potential as soon as adequate 2 d detector arrays become available. Because of the moderate geometric resolution of about 250 m pixel size from a Polar Platform (PoP) orbital altitude of 824 km, the main application will be for ocean colour measurements in open waters and coastal zones. Survey type monitoring of the vegetation status over land surfaces such as crop status or extended forest disease detection will be in the range of application as well. In addition, objectives such as cloud top height derivation through the 02 bands at 750 770 nm or aerosol load determination should be in the ROSIS application range. In general, the instrument defining measurement task of the chlorophyll fluorescence and absorption detection is considered as one of the most challenging spaceborne remote sensing task, since: - the ocean surface represents the "darkest" natural surface on the Earth - the ocean surface signal is buried in by far exceeding signals from the atmosphere (Rayleigh and Mie-backscatter) - The fluorescence peak at 685 nm represents a rather small "delta signal" at low chlorophyll concentration. It was not the least the need for a high optical throughput which originated the ROSIS concept of a purely reflective concept. The summary requirements are presented in Tab. 1. 2. ROSIS as Candidate Instrument for Polar Platform MissionsThe current planning by ESA, NASA and other Agencies for the Polar Platform (PoP) Missions (see Fig. 5 for the ESA plat-form) to start in the last half decade of this century include two types of imaging spectrometers:- high spatial resolution /small FOV IS (HRIS in ESA planning, HIRIS in NASA planning) with 30 -60 km swath, ixelsize 30 -50 m, 0.A < 10 nm- medium spatial resolution /larger FOV IS (MERIS IN ESA, MODIS in NASA planning * *) with 1000 - 1500 kmswath, < 500 m pixel size, AA < 5 nm for MERISAccording to the ROSIS performance specifications of Tab. 3, it is obvious that the combination of e.g. 2 ROSIS optics moduleswith a total of 2024/4048 pixels would perfectly meet the main MERIS requirements, with the exception of an extented spectralrange from 390 to 1050 nm. This extension is principally feasible from the optics and FPA point -of -view, the critical area is thesecond order (of the blazed grating) suppression which currently is specified to about 98 %. Whether this is sufficient, is subjectof a dedicated ESA study. Compared to MODIS, there are two versions planned. MODIS -T (= Tilt) with similar characteristicsas for ROSIS, but a different technique, i.e. applying the "whisk- broom" technique, which means use of a mechanical scannerfor spatial scanning while ROSIS is a genuine electronical scanner. MODIS -N would include IR channels as well, referred to asROSIS -P in Tab. 5.Yet also the high - resolution versions can be considered with a rather modified optical concept: the telescope would then becomethe larger optical element while the spectrometer part would remain at about the same size, see Tab. 5. ROSIS as Candidate Instrument for Polar Platform Missions The current planning by ESA, NASA and other Agencies for the Polar Platform (PoP) Missions (see Fig. 5 for the ESA plat­ form) to start in the last half decade of this century include two types of imaging spectrometers: - high spatial resolution/small FOV IS (HRIS in ESA planning, HIRIS in NASA planning) with 30-60 km swath, ixel size 30-50 m, A A < 10 nm *) - medium spatial resolution/larger FOV IS (MERIS IN ESA, MODIS in NASA planning **) with 1000 - 1500 km swath, < 500 m pixel size, AA < 5 nm for MERIS According to the ROSIS performance specifications of Tab. 3, it is obvious that the combination of e.g. 2 ROSIS optics modules with a total of 2024/4048 pixels would perfectly meet the main MERIS requirements, with the exception of an extented spectral range from 390 to 1050 nm. This extension is principally feasible from the optics and FPA point-of-view, the critical area is the second order (of the blazed grating) suppression which currently is specified to about 98%. Whether this is sufficient, is subject of a dedicated ESA study. Compared to MODIS, there are two versions planned: MODIS-T (= Tilt) with similar characteristics as for ROSIS, but a different technique, i.e. applying the "whisk-broom" technique, which means use of a mechanical scanner for spatial scanning while ROSIS is a genuine electronical scanner. MODIS-N would include IR channels as well, referred to as ROSIS-P in Tab. 5. Yet also the high-resolution versions can be considered with a rather modified optical concept: the telescope would then become the larger optical element while the spectrometer part would remain at about the same size, see Tab. 5. The applicability of this concept to also the HRIS type instrument is subject of another ESA Study. 7. Acknowledgements The authors like to express their particular acknowledgements to Dr. R.
