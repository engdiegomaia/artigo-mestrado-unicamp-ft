I NTRODUCTION AS A REVOLUTIONARY breakthrough in Earth observation, hyperspectral remote sensing technology is fundamentally transforming human understanding of the world by offering unparalleled precision along with a broad range of applications [1]. Unlike conventional multispectral remote sensing, hyperspectral remote sensing provides a superior degree of detail, enabling the detection of subtle biochemistry changes and precise representation of the spectral properties of various ground features [2]. Consequently, hyperspectral remote sensing technology has emerged as a crucial method for studying ground surface ecology and serves as a vital source of information for comprehending the Earth’s systems. Its applications extend widely across environmental monitoring [3], mineral identiﬁcation [4], oil spill detection [5],[6], urban surveillance [7], and precision agriculture [8]. However, in certain monitoring applications, the macroscale observation capabilities of satellite-borne hyperspectral images often conﬂict with the ﬁner geographical scale [9].F o ri n - stance, in precision agriculture, obtaining crop canopy attribute information and detecting pest and disease require monitoring ground subject at a ﬁner grained spatial scale, a level of detail that is challenging to achieve with spaceborne hyperspectral data. Consequently, improving the spatial resolution of HSIs is crucial for attaining a comprehensive understanding of the geometric spatial distribution and physical characteristics of ground surface feature. This improvement enables more precise classiﬁcations, thereby laying a solid foundation for quantitative remote sensing and supporting applications, such as crop yield estimation [10], vegetation nitrogen content estimation [11], and carbon stock assessment [12]. In recent years, rapid advancements in unmanned aerial vehicle (UA V) technology, coupled with the miniaturization of hyperspectral imaging equipment, have made UA V hyperspectral remote sensing become an essential instrument for precise Earth observation [13]. UA V H YPERSPECTRAL OBSERV ATION PLATFORMS AND DATASOURCES The UA V hyperspectral observation platform primarily consists of three components: the UA V payload platform, the lightweight hyperspectral imager, and the auxiliary integrationequipment. The auxiliary integration equipment includes a microcomputer system for data acquisition and storage, a Global Navigation Satellite System module for recording exposure time and positional information, and an inertial measurement unit module for capturing attitude information. This article primarily focuses on reviewing UA V payload platforms and lightweight hyperspectral imager systems. A. UAV Payload Platforms Unmanned platforms predominantly refer to UA Vs, which are extensively utilized in military, agricultural, mapping, environmental monitoring, and other ﬁelds [23]. UA V payload platforms are generally selected based on factors, such as ﬂight altitude, range, agility, and payload capacity. They can be primarily categorized into three types: ﬁxed-wing UA Vs, multirotor UA Vs, and vertical take-off and landing (VTOL) ﬁxed-wing UA Vs. However, they frequently require extensive take-off and landing space, as they rely on runways or catapult launch mechanisms, which increases the difﬁculty of data acquisition and limits their potential of UA Vs remote sensing applications. However, their intricate structure raises operation and maintenance expenses, and they typically have reduced payload capacity. UA V H YPERSPECTRAL IMAGE CLASSIFICATION METHODS AND APPLICATIONS Traditional classiﬁcation methods, which primarily focus on spectral feature extraction and statistical models, have long been prevalent in UA V hyperspectral image classiﬁcation. However, the emergence of deep learning techniques [34] has revolutionized UA V hyperspectral classiﬁcation, driving signiﬁcant advancements in recent years [35]. This section outlines the common classiﬁcation techniques for UA V hyperspectral image and highlights their practical applications in research. It demonstrates the transformative impact of deep learning on the accuracy and efﬁciency of hyperspectral classiﬁcation. A. Conventional Hyperspectral Image Classiﬁcation Methods Conventional HSI classiﬁcation methods primarily hinge on spectral feature extraction and classiﬁer. These approaches often employ manual feature design combined with statistical learning algorithms to achieve the classiﬁcation task. Despite the surge of deep learning in HSI classiﬁcation, traditional machine learning algorithms remain highly competitive in various applications. One prominent method is sparse coding, a signal reconstruction technique that classiﬁes hyperspectral pixels by representing each pixel as a sparse linear combination within a predeﬁned dictionary. Its efﬁcacy in managing high-dimensional hyperspectral features while mitigating redundant information makes sparse coding widely applicable. For instance, Nie et al. [36] introduced a novel structure-prior constrained low-rank and sparse representation with discriminative incremental dictionary method,which enhances classiﬁcation by integrating discriminative dictionaries and incorporating structural constraints. This approach effectively captures the structural features of HSIs, thereby augmenting classiﬁcation robustness and accuracy. Compressed sensing is another prevalent technique employed to recover sparse signals from limited measurement data. This method is particularly useful for feature extraction and dimensionality reduction in HSI classiﬁcation. By minimizing data redundancy, compressed sensing helps to address the high dimensionality challenges inherent in hyperspectral data. For instance, Vargas and Arguello [37] proposed a method that integrates compressed sensing with feature extraction by leveraging a low-rank matrix approximation model, enabling efﬁcient feature compression while maintaining strong classiﬁcation performance through sparse representation and low-rank signal approximation. Kernel learning methods offer a different approach by mapping raw data to a high-dimensional feature space through nonlinear transformations, followed by linear classiﬁcation [38]. Kernel learning techniques are well-suited for handling the nonlinear features in HSIs, yielding strong performance in classiﬁcation tasks. Mirzaei [39] proposed a method combining K-plane clustering and kernel principal component analysis (KPCA) for HSI classiﬁcation. The approach ﬁrst applies K-plane clustering to group the data, followed by KPCA to extract nonlinear features in the kernel space, ultimately leading to efﬁcient classiﬁcation. This method effectively addresses the complex nonlinear relationships inherent in hyperspectral data. Support vector machine (SVM) is a classical supervised learning method extensively applied in HSI classiﬁcation. Its ability to handle high-dimensional data and effectively capture nonlinear features by selecting appropriate kernel functions makes it highly effective in this domain. SVM separates different data classes by ﬁnding an optimal hyperplane demonstrating strong generalization capabilities and robust performance in hyperspectral classiﬁcation tasks. Numerous studies have shown that SVM can achieve high classiﬁcation accuracy with hyperspectral data, particularly when dealing with relatively small datasets [40]. Compared totraditional machine learning methods, deep learning can automatically learn complex spectral and spatial features from highdimensional data, making it particularly well-suited for handling rich spectral information and intricate nonlinear relationships present in HSIs. Deep learning methods for HSI classiﬁcation substantially enhance classiﬁcation accuracy and broaden the scope of their practical application. Based on the network architectures, UA V hyperspectral image classiﬁcation networks can be categorized into CNN-based models, transformer-based models, RNN-based models, GCN-based models, GAN-based models, and hybrid models. In addition, hybrid models are emerging as signiﬁcant research directions in this domain. 1) Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) for UAV HSI Classiﬁcation: CNNs [46] are among the most commonly used deep learning architectures for efﬁciently capturing spatial– spectral features in images. They extract features layer-by-layer through convolutional, pooling, and fully connected processing, ultimately outputting optimal classiﬁcation results using the --- Página 10 --- 3108 IEEE JOURNAL OF SELECTED TOPICS IN APPLIED EARTH OBSERVATIONS AND REMOTE SENSING, VOL. 18, 2025 softmax function. For instance, the 1-D CNNs (1D-CNNs) [47], which focus on spectral information feature extraction, utilize 1-D spectral features as inputs and progressively extract spectral information. This approach is similar to other spectral feature extraction networks, such as stacked autoencoders [48] and deep belief networks [49],[50], which also process 1-D vector data but emphasize spectral information while neglecting spatial feature extraction. In contrast, 2-D CNNs (2D-CNNs) [51] concentrate more on the spatial information surrounding the target pixels. These networks typically achieve HSI feature extraction by leveraging principal component analysis (PCA) or minimum noise fraction (MNF) dimensionality reduction, combined with 2-D convolutional layers. However, both 1D-CNNs and 2DCNNs fall short in fully utilizing the combined spatial–spectral features inherent in HSIs. To address this limitation, researchers have developed 3-D CNNs (3D-CNNs) [52] that simultaneously perform convolutional operations across both spatial and spectral dimensions. This makes 3D-CNNs a commonly employed and effective technique for HSI classiﬁcation. As CNN networks deepen, the classiﬁcation models tend to introduce multitudinous training parameters, increasing the risk of overﬁtting. To address this issue, researchers have proposed various strategies to enhance CNNs performance, particularly by incorporating the attention mechanism, supplementing residual mechanism, and optimizing network structure. For instance, attention mechanisms methods, such as self-attention [53], convolutional block attention module (CBAM) [54], and ECA [55], dynamically generate weights for distinguishing the importance of different input features. Conventional Transformers are composed of four main components: the input, encoder, decoder, and the output. The output is a linear layer that produces probabilistic predictions through a softmax function. VI. C ONCLUSION Over the past decades, UA V hyperspectral remote sensing image classiﬁcation techniques have achieved remarkable advancements, offering unparalleled detail and precision for sophisticated Earth observation. This article reviews the applications and performances of traditional machine learning and deep learning-based methods in this ﬁeld, and offers a summary of the main current research directions and technical challenges. Traditional machine learning algorithms (e.g., sparse coding, compressed sensing, and kernel methods) can achieve certain levels of classiﬁcation accuracy. However, they often struggle with the high dimensionality and nonlinear nature of hyperspectral data. Speciﬁcally, in complex application scenarios, traditional methods often fail to capture the intricate relationship between spectral information and spatial features, thereby limiting their practical applications. Currently, deep learning techniques, such as CNN-based methods, RNN-based methods, Transformer-based methods, and GCN-based methods, demonstrate strong potential in HSI classiﬁcation, and these models can signiﬁcantly improve the accuracy and robustness of classiﬁcation by exploiting spatial– spectral features. Meanwhile, the adoption of hybrid networkarchitectures (e.g., combining CNNs and GCNs) further enhances the model’s adaptability and performance various scenarios. Nevertheless, these models face substantial computational demands and rely heavily on large training datasets, presenting ongoing technical bottlenecks for their real-time deployment. The performance of different neural network frameworks in UA V hyperspectral image classiﬁcation varies signiﬁcantly, with GCN-based and CNN-based methods generally outperforming RNN-based approaches. The advantage of GCN-based methods becomes particularly pronounced when dealing with small sample sizes. Despite these advancements, current UA V hyperspectral image classiﬁcation techniques still encounter several challenges, including high-dimensional data processing, spectrum heterogeneity, and radiometric variability across different scenes. Furthermore, deep learning remains largely a “closed box” solution for most problems. However, with the integration of artiﬁcial intelligence, deep learning, and Big Data technologies, UA V hyperspectral remote sensing is transitioning from a passive “observer” to an active “analyzer” and “decision maker.” This evolution positions UA V hyperspectral technology not only as a cutting-edge technology in remote sensing, but also as a key driver in the era of intelligent Earth observation, elevating our data processing and analysis capabilities to unprecedented levels.
