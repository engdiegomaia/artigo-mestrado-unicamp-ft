Clearance and land release efforts anchored on manual geophysical detection and mechanical probing methods remain painstakingly slow, expensive, and dangerous to operators. As a result, postconflict regions impacted by EO contamination significantly lag in social and economic develop - ment. Developing, calibrating, and field testing more efficient detection methods for surficial EO is a crucial task. Unpiloted aerial systems featuring advanced remote sensing capabilities are a key technology that may allow the tide to turn in the EO crisis. Specifically, recent advances in hardware design have allowed for effective deployment of small, light, and less power consuming hyperspectral imaging (HSI) systems from small unpiloted aerial vehicles (UAVs). Our proof-of-concept study employs UAV-based HSI to deliver a safer, faster, and more cost-efficient method of surface land mine and ERW detection compared to current ground-based detection methods. Our results indicate that analysis of HSI data sets can produce spectral profiles and derivative data products to distinguish multiple ERW and mine types in a variety of host environments. Introduction Remnant explosive ordnance (EO) represents a significant detriment to social and economic recovery in postconflict nations worldwide. EO killed or injured 7073 people worldwide in 2020. In 2020, an estimated 135,000 antipersonnel mines were destroyed and 146 km2 of contaminated land was released (International Campaign to Ban Landmines, 2021). Although these numbers are encouraging, it is estimated that there are more than 100 million pieces of EO worldwide (de Smet and Nikulin, 2018; de Smet et al., 2018; Nikulin et al., 2018, 2020). Moreover, it is estimated that 10 to 20 land mines are laid for every mine cleared (Dolgov, 2001), and this number may have risen recently given the current conflict in Eastern Europe. It must be noted here, however, that since the implementation of the Ottawa Treaty, more than 30 countries have become mine free (International Campaign to Ban Landmines, 2020). The HALO Trust has Madison Tuohy1, Jasper Baur2,3, Gabriel Steinberg3, Jalissa Pirro1, Taylor Mitchell4, Alex Nikulin1,3,5, John Frucci4, and Timothy S. de Smet1,3,5 recently quantified the impacts of EO and noted that antivehicle and antipersonnel land mines are the leading cause of civilian casualties in Ukraine (Mathewson, 2022). At current rates of clearance, it will take 1100 years to rid the world of land mines (Khamis, 2015). Simply put, at the current rate of detection and clearance, the global EO crisis is not getting better; it is getting worse every day. It is thus imperative that new technologies are tested and implemented to increase the efficiency of EO detection. Terrestrial time-domain electromagnetic induction (EMI) is currently the standard geophysical methodology that humanitarian mine action (HMA) survey operators rely on to detect subsurface EO (Habib, 2007). Handheld coiled EMI metal detectors have been used in demining for more than 100 years and are a reliable, proven technology (Fails et al., 2008). However, EMI surveys have several key limitations: they are slow, labor intensive, and expensive; they have high false-alarm rates due to metallic clutter; they pose a significant risk to operators; and they cannot be used to find modern low-metal-content plastic land mines. These limitations considerably extend the time frames for HMA clear - ance and land release activity and ultimately impede social and economic development in postconflict nations. While EMI surveys will likely remain the trusted methodology of technical manual land mine clearance and land release, other methodologies are emerging in the realm of so-called area-reduction methods that aim to identify areas of EO contamination rather than individual EO elements. Unpiloted aerial vehicles (UAVs), also known as drones, are a rapidly emerging tool for remote detection of EO-contaminated areas. Over the last decade, UAV technology has advanced to the point that surveys are highly automated and are not labor-intensive. UAV systems paired with modern remote sensors are fast, safe, and labor efficient. Recent advances in hardware design have significantly decreased the size, weight, and power (SWaP) consumption, allowing for much smaller and less expensive remote sensors. Specifically, SWaP of HSI sensors has decreased such that they are now compatible with small UAVs. Our proof-of-concept study combines UAV-based HSI in an attempt to develop and calibrate a safer, faster, and more costefficient method of remote surficial EO detection as compared to current ground-based detection methods. To accomplish this task, we relied on the facilities of the Oklahoma State University Center for Fire and Explosives, Forensic Investigation, Training and Research (CENFEX), which hosts a large collection of inert EO and allows for their seeding in different host environments. We surficially emplaced multiple pieces of EO, including antipersonnel mines, antivehicle mines, rockets, missiles, and grenades. Significantly, several of these EO types represent munitions most commonly used in the conflict in Ukraine (GICHD, 2022; Mathewson, 2022), including the TM-62M and TM-62P3 antivehicle mines; 40 mm submunitions; OZM-72, PFM-1, PMN, and PMN-4 antipersonnel mines; 82 mm mortars; multiple types of cluster bombs; 122 mm Grad rockets; PG-7 rocket-propelled grenades (RPGs); and several fuses. Data were collected in simu - lated weapons-contaminated areas over several different environ - mental settings with a DJI Phantom 4 Pro 20-megapixel visible light camera, a DJI Phantom 3 with a Parrot Sequoia multispectral camera, a DJI Mavic 2 Enterprise Dual visible light and thermal camera, and a Corning microHSI 410 selectable hyperspectral airborne remote sensing kit (SHARK) attached to a DJI Ronin-MX gimbal on a DJI Matrice 600. Airborne hyperspectral data were collected over three types of inert surficial weapons-contaminated areas from 7 June through 10 June 2022. The three different environments consisted of bare ground with minimal vegetation, short grasses (less than 0.3 m in height), and tall grasses (approxi - mately 1 m in height). We emplaced multiple rows of EO, including antipersonnel mines, antivehicle mines, rockets, missiles, and grenades, in each of these environments. Visible light RGB orthomosaics were produced from the DJI Mavic 2 and Phantom 4 cameras. Green, red, red-edge, and near-infrared orthomosaics were produced from the Parrot Sequoia camera. All flights were flown at 10 m elevation above ground level, resulting in a ground sampling distance (GSD) of less than 2 mm for the visible light orthomosaics and 1 cm for HSI. Data were collected on clear days within an hour of solar noon. Data collected at such low elevation will have no atmospheric interfer - ence such as path radiance. It should be noted here that the fine GSD comes at the cost of coverage. Ground control points (GCPs) were placed at the corners and center of the approximately 25 × 10 m grids. A Trimble Geo 7X global navigation satellite antenna was used to collect subcentimeter-level global positioning data over the GCPs to georeference the orthomosaics. All visible light and multispectral data were processed with PIX4Dmapper, while HSI data were processed with ENVI software from Harris Geospatial. Data herein are visualized in ESRI’s ArcGIS software. Raw 2x binned data, or digital numbers, were calibrated to radiance (W/m2/sr/μm) with a dark subtraction and nonuniformity cor - rection. Kodak gray cards, WhiBal, and a MicaSense-calibrated reflectance panel were in the collected imagery data and flown over at 10 m AGL. Radiance data were converted to reflectance with a per-band empirical line method. The development of standards for the characterization of HSI cameras is important to quantitative analyses of these data (Ientilucci et al., 2022). HSI optical design As previously mentioned, the miniaturization of HSI has revolutionized the remote detection of surficial EO by making it feasible to mount HSI systems on UAVs. The unique design of the Corning microHSI sensor was what gave the instrument the ability to drastically decrease in size by two times and operate at a faster speed than the previous prototype version (Warren et al., 2012). The Offner spectrometer series, which was implemented in the Corning microHSI sensor, created a smaller spectrometer size reduction by blazed, confract differentiation grating based on an Offner relay (Ziph-Schatzberg et al., 2015). The collected and dispersed light in a medium with a high refractive index allows for a smaller sensor volume, as compared to traditional HSI sensors that utilize classic air-based mirrors. In turn, Corning successfully produced a small, monolithic HSI sensor with excep - tional image quality and spectral precision that can be mounted on a UAV and collects 150 relatively narrow bands (ZiphSchatzberg et al., 2015). An HSI camera measures reflectance of very fine bandwidths (approximately 2–4 nm), as opposed to a multispectral camera with bandwidths that are often an order of magnitude or orders of magnitude larger (approximately 10–40 nm on the Parrot Sequoia). The development of the Corning microHSI 410 SHARK allows for feasible mounting of HSI on a UAV due to its low SWaP. The MicroHSI 410 SHARK’s data were processed using ENVI software by Harris Geospatial. HSI contains a massive amount of data in narrow bandwidths, so a principal component analysis (PCA) is often used to reduce the dimensionality of the data and convert the data from highly correlated reflectance to uncorrelated data on axes of variance. By reducing the dimensional - ity of correlated data, the PCA can help effectively distinguish desired patterns, here the physical signatures of distinct mines. Results and discussion Figure 1 shows PG-7 RPGs from the three environments where data were collected, namely short grass under 0.3 m, bare ground silty clay, and tall grass greater than 1.0 m. The RPGs can be seen easily in each environment from visible light imagery from the DJI Mavic 2 and Phantom 4 UAVs. The spectral profiles in Figure 2 illustrate another strength of HSI — its ability to differentiate between EO and host geology and vegetation environ - ments based on their reflectance. These profiles prove useful in identifying the locations of EO in the environments in which they were emplaced and demonstrate an aspect of HSI that is unique to multispectral imaging and HSI. With the reflectance values of EO varying highly from the locations in which they were placed, the mines can be discriminated from not only their host environments but from each other as well. In turn, the explosive remnants of war (ERW) can be detected as well as identified by their specific compositions and types due to their unique spectral profiles. In addition to the successes of spectral profiles and HSI pseudo RGBs, the use of a PCA proved to be valuable as well. Analysis of the main contributing eigenvectors and geometry of PC band 7 from the bare ground environment was successful in identifying PFM-1 antipersonnel land mines. Other images from the optical sensors have shown to be the same or worse than the PCA at discriminating EO, specifically PFM-1 anipersonnel land mines, from their surrounding environments. This indicates that the success rate of HSI is higher in imaging EO compared to previous methods of demining strategies. A major implication of these results is the possible future integration of HSI data sets into a convolutional neural network (CNN) currency being developed for rapid analysis of remote sensing data sets for presence of EO objects (Baur et al., 2020). Demining researchers are focusing primarily on harnessing machine learning to create a CNN, which is an algorithm that can be trained to recognize specific EO types. To train the algo - rithm, a large number of images containing the EO must be presented to it. The CNN is then able to pick out the distinguishing features of the desired objects we are looking to detect. As previ - ously mentioned, a distinguishing feature of hyperspectral data is that they contain shorter bandwidths, which in turn generates more bands. This is extremely useful for the CNN because a large amount of data can be added to it in the form of training sets, or images that can be used as a basis for what to distinguish in the future. With a large amount of data for the CNN, the algorithm can be better trained to distinguish EO and become more accurate. Previous research has shown that a CNN on visible light images can attain 92% accuracy to detect the PFM-1 surficial antiperson - nel land mine as an object, a cluster of pixels as opposed to a single pixel (Baur et al., 2021); however, additional channels from other remote sensors can potentially allow for the detection of more types of mines and a reduction of false alarms due to clutter. We intend to add the PCA of the PFM-1 land mines as a new channel to the CNN to further increase the accuracy of the network. Conclusion and future research The use of HSI mounted onto UAVs will allow for advancements in automated EO detection. The abilities of the HSI, as well as the sensor’s miniaturization, low SWaP, and availability, will be an important addition to demining research. Comparing this sensor to previous methods of EO detection, HSI has the capability to success - fully produce distinguishing spec - tral profiles and distinguish EO in a variety of environments that have been evasive in the past. Furthermore, adding these data sets as new channels to machine learning and artificial intelligence, specifically CNNs, will assist in creating a more accurate and rapid automated detection of a far larger variety of land mines. We will conduct unstructured and structured target detection of the HSI data to compare with the CNN we have been developing over the last several years; this will include the potential for subpixel detection (Cañas et al., 2022) versus the object-based group of pixel detection methods we have employed previously. Moreover, we will conduct future field trials at CENFEX that will expand the number and types of EO; add additional UAVbased geophysical and remote sensing methods for additional CNN channels and broaden use of the electromagnetic spectrum (i.e., LiDAR, ground-penetrating radar, magnetometry, timedomain EMI); and add buried EO to see both depth of investiga - tion and the impact of ground disturbance on detection. Development, calibration, and implementation of new technologies is crucial to detect rapidly evolving EO as they have become more dangerous and difficult to detect. These findings can complement other common detection methods to increase the accuracy of detection in area reduction in technical surveys. In our case, lower resolution did not pose a massive issue because we were still able to successfully identify EO. The midsize to larger EO, such as RPGs, were rela - tively easy to see regardless of the coarser data. Smaller land mines, such as the PFM-1 antipersonnel land mine, were also imaged successfully, further demonstrating that the coarseness of the imaging is not a major concern. Another difficulty presented in airborne HSI is windowing. When HSI is mounted onto a UAV, data are collected using a pushbroom measurement approach, meaning that a line of spectral data is collected per exposure. Windowing occurs when the collected lines do not successfully correspond, leaving unintegrated scenes or discontinuities in the data (Stuart et al., 2019). Similar to the GSD, windowing does not pose a massive limitation for this specific use. Windowing did occur occasionally in our collected data; however, no land mines remained undetected due to this phenomenon. Therefore, the limitations that are commonly present in HSI do not appear to be of high importance or concern when remotely imaging EO. It is valuable to consider the possibility of these limitations potentially rendering results in the future. Finally, a fundamental limitation of the methodology is the possibility of line-of-sight obstruction (or occlusion), typically by vegetation or anthropogenic clutter. While the first case can be remedied by conducting the survey during low-vegetation seasons, such as early spring or late fall, anthropogenic clutter or intentional camouflaging of EO stockpiles would be a challenge in deploying this methodology.
